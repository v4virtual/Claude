Five habits that win marks

1

Finish the job

Multiply the numbers. Put the same letters together. Anything to the power 0 is 1.

2

Root first

\(a^{\frac{m}{n}}\): take the \(n\)-th root first, then the power. Small numbers, fewer slips.

3

Flip only when crossing

A negative exponent turns positive only if that factor moves to the other side of the fraction line.

4

Stay in exponent form

Turn \(\sqrt{x}\) into \(x^{\frac12}\) first. Add and subtract exponents instead of building fractions.

5

Same base

Write \(8\) as \(2^3\), \(27\) as \(3^3\), \(16\) as \(2^4\). Then the laws can do the work.

Four classic slips

These are the mistakes that cost the most marks in exponent tests. Each one has a quick fix.

1. Stopping halfway

Slip
\(5t^4g^{-2}\times2tg^2=5t^4\tfrac{1}{g^2}\cdot2tg^2\)
Fix
\(=10\,t^{4+1}g^{-2+2}=10t^5\)

Numbers multiply, exponents of the same letter add, and \(g^0=1\). The answer is not finished until each letter appears once.

2. Power before root

Slip
\(36^{\frac32}=\sqrt{36^3}=\sqrt{46\,656}\)
Fix
\(36^{\frac32}=\left(\sqrt{36}\right)^3=6^3=216\)

Both orders are correct math, but cubing first gives a huge number that is hard to square-root without a calculator.

3. Flipping a sign without moving

Slip
\(\dfrac{9y^{-4}}{x^{-2}}=\dfrac{9y^{4}}{x^{2}}\)
Fix
\(\dfrac{9y^{-4}}{x^{-2}}=\dfrac{9x^{2}}{y^{4}}\)

A negative exponent only becomes positive when its factor crosses the fraction line. No move, no flip.

4. Big numbers instead of the same base

Slow
\(\left(\dfrac{2^8}{8^2}\right)^{-\frac12}=\left(\dfrac{256}{64}\right)^{-\frac12}=4^{-\frac12}\)
Fast
\(\left(\dfrac{2^8}{2^6}\right)^{-\frac12}=\left(2^2\right)^{-\frac12}=2^{-1}=\tfrac12\)

Writing \(8^2\) as \(2^6\) keeps the numbers small. Level 7–8 questions expect this route.

Lesson 1 of 8

A power is repeated multiplication

The base is the number being multiplied. The exponent counts how many copies of it you multiply together.

25
= 32

Press + and − to change the exponent. Each extra copy doubles the value.

Multiplying powers: just count the tiles

aaa × aa = aaaaa

\(a^3\times a^2\): 3 copies and 2 more copies make 5 copies, so \(a^5\).

Product law
\(a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}\)

Same base, multiplying: add the exponents. The base does not change: \(2^3\times2^4=2^7\), not \(4^7\).

Two quiet traps

A letter on its own has exponent 1: \(t=t^1\). So \(t^4\times t=t^5\).
Different bases do not combine: \(x^3\times y^2\) stays as \(x^3y^2\).

Lesson 2 of 8

Dividing is canceling

A copy on top and a copy underneath cancel each other out, because \(\frac{a}{a}=1\).

aaaaa aa = aaa

\(\dfrac{a^5}{a^2}\): two pairs cancel, three copies are left, so \(a^3\).

Quotient law
\(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\)

Same base, dividing: subtract the exponents (top minus bottom). Numbers in front are divided as normal numbers: \(\frac{8}{2}=4\).

Why anything to the power 0 is 1

aaa aaa =1

Everything cancels, so the answer is 1. The law says \(a^{3-3}=a^0\). Both must be true, so \(a^0=1\).

Finish the job

Sort the expression into piles: numbers with numbers, and each letter with its own kind.

\(5t^4g^{-2}\times 2tg^2\)
Numbers
\(5\times2=10\)
t pile
\(t^4\times t^1=t^5\)
g pile
\(g^{-2}\times g^{2}=g^{0}=1\)
\(=10t^5\)
Before you box your answer

Is there only one number in front? Does each letter appear only once? Are all exponents natural numbers (no 0, no negatives)? If not, keep going.

Lesson 3 of 8

A power of a power multiplies

aa aa aa = aaaaaa

\((a^2)^3\) means \(a^2\times a^2\times a^2\): 3 groups of 2 copies, so \(2\times3=6\) copies, \(a^6\).

Power of a power
\((a^m)^n=a^{m\times n}\)

A bracket with a power outside: multiply the exponents. For example \((x^5)^2=x^{10}\).

Everything inside the bracket gets the power

\((2x^3)^2=(2x^3)(2x^3)=2^2x^{6}=4x^6\)
\((x^{-2}y^{5})^{-3}=x^{(-2)(-3)}\,y^{(5)(-3)}=x^{6}y^{-15}\)

The number in front is powered too. Signs follow normal multiplication: negative times negative is positive.

Common mix-ups

\((x^4)^3=x^{12}\), not \(x^{7}\). Adding is only for \(x^4\times x^3\).
\((3x)^2=9x^2\), not \(3x^2\). The 3 is inside the bracket, so it is squared as well.

Lesson 4 of 8

Negative exponents make fractions, not negative numbers

Walk down the powers of 2. Every step to the right divides by 2. There is no reason to stop at 1.

422
221
120
1/22−1
1/42−2
1/82−3

Drawn to scale. Each bar is half the one before. \(2^{-3}\) is small (\(\frac18\)) but still positive.

Negative exponent
\(a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}\qquad\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-n}=\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{n}\)

The minus sign means "take the reciprocal". \(4^{-2}=\frac{1}{16}\), never \(-16\). For a fraction, flip it and make the power positive: \(\left(\frac23\right)^{-2}=\left(\frac32\right)^{2}=\frac94\).

The lift rule: flip the sign only when you cross the line

y−4x−2 → x2y4

Each factor took the lift to the other floor, so its sign flipped.

The most common slip

Writing \(9y^{-4}\) as \(9y^{4}\) while it stays on top is wrong: no move, no flip. If it stays on top it must be written \(\frac{1}{y^4}\) or kept as \(y^{-4}\).

Even easier: don't move anything

Keep the negative exponents and use the quotient law. Watch the double minus:

\(\dfrac{y^{-4}}{y^{-20}}=y^{-4-(-20)}=y^{-4+20}=y^{16}\)
Lesson 5 of 8

Fractional exponents are roots in disguise

Why is \(a^{\frac12}\) the same as \(\sqrt{a}\)? Use the product law:

\(a^{\frac12}\times a^{\frac12}=a^{\frac12+\frac12}=a^{1}=a\)

So \(a^{\frac12}\) is the number that gives \(a\) when you multiply it by itself. That is exactly what \(\sqrt{a}\) means. In the same way, \(a^{\frac13}=\sqrt[3]{a}\) because three copies multiply to \(a\).

Flower on top, roots underground

The fraction line is the ground. The top number is the power; the bottom number is the root.

Fractional exponent
\(a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^{m}\)

Both orders give the same answer, but root first keeps the numbers small.

Root first vs power first

Power first
\(36^3=46\,656\)
\(\sqrt{46\,656}=\;?\)

Huge numbers, long multiplication, easy to slip, and the square root is very hard without a calculator.

Root first
\(\sqrt{36}=6\)
\(6^3=216\)

Small numbers you can do in your head. Same answer.

Turn roots into exponents before anything else

\(\sqrt{x}=x^{\frac12}\)
\(\sqrt[3]{x}=x^{\frac13}\)
\(\sqrt{x^3}=x^{\frac32}\)

Then a messy bracket becomes easy:

\(\left(x^{-2}\times\sqrt{x}\right)^{-4}=\left(x^{-2}\times x^{\frac12}\right)^{-4}\)
\(=\left(x^{-\frac32}\right)^{-4}=x^{6}\)

−2 + ½ = −1½, then −1½ × −4 = 6

Lesson 6 of 8

Make the bases match

The laws only work when the bases are the same. So when you see 8 next to 2, or 27 next to 3, rewrite the bigger number as a power of the smaller one. Learn these by heart:

Base 2 22¹42²82³162⁴322⁵642⁶1282⁷2562⁸
Base 3 33¹93²273³813⁴2433⁵
Base 5 55¹255²1255³6255⁴

Example: the Level 7 way

  1. \(\left(\dfrac{2^8}{8^2}\right)^{-\frac12}=\left(\dfrac{2^8}{(2^3)^2}\right)^{-\frac12}\)

    Write 8 as \(2^3\). Now everything is base 2.

  2. \(=\left(\dfrac{2^8}{2^6}\right)^{-\frac12}=\left(2^{2}\right)^{-\frac12}\)

    Power of a power, then the quotient law. No big numbers.

  3. \(=2^{2\times(-\frac12)}=2^{-1}=\dfrac12\)

    Multiply the exponents. A negative exponent means reciprocal.

Letters in the exponent? Same trick

\(4^{n+1}=\left(2^2\right)^{n+1}=2^{2(n+1)}=2^{2n+2}\)

Multiply the whole bracket by 2, not just the \(n\).

Equations with powers

If both sides have the same base, the exponents must be equal:

\(3^{2x}=3^{x+4}\ \Rightarrow\ 2x=x+4\ \Rightarrow\ x=4\)
Lesson 7 of 8

Scientific notation keeps the number between 1 and 10

The form
\(a\times10^{n}\quad\text{with}\quad 1\le a\lt 10\)

Exactly one non-zero digit before the decimal point.

The see-saw

If the number in front gets 10 times smaller, the power of 10 must get one bigger, so the value stays the same:

\(78.352\times10^{1}=7.8352\times10^{2}\)

Multiplying in scientific notation

  1. \((4\times10^{5})\times(6\times10^{-2})=(4\times6)\times10^{5+(-2)}\)

    Numbers together, powers of 10 together (product law).

  2. \(=24\times10^{3}\)

    Not finished: 24 is bigger than 10.

  3. \(=2.4\times10^{1}\times10^{3}=2.4\times10^{4}\)

    Fix the front number, then add 1 to the exponent.

Lesson 8 of 8

All the laws on one page

Product\(a^m\times a^n=a^{m+n}\)
Quotient\(\dfrac{a^m}{a^n}=a^{m-n}\)
Power of a power\((a^m)^n=a^{mn}\)
Power of a product\((ab)^n=a^nb^n\)
Zero\(a^0=1\)
Negative\(a^{-n}=\dfrac{1}{a^n}\)
Flip a fraction\(\left(\dfrac{a}{b}\right)^{-n}=\left(\dfrac{b}{a}\right)^{n}\)
Fractional\(a^{\frac{m}{n}}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m\)
Same base\(a^x=a^y\Rightarrow x=y\)

A good order of attack for big questions

  1. Turn every root into a fractional exponent. Rewrite numbers with a common base (8 → 2³).

  2. Get rid of brackets: multiply exponents, and power the numbers inside too.

  3. Deal with each pile separately: numbers, then each letter. Add exponents for top × top, subtract bottom from top.

  4. Only at the very end: turn any negative exponent into a positive one by moving it across the fraction line.

Lesson 1 of 1

Six rules for decimals

Each rule fixes one detail that is easy to miss. In practice, every answer shows the full column working with the decimal points lined up.

Comparing

Give both numbers the same number of decimal places, then compare from the left.

0.5 vs 0.45 → 0.50 vs 0.45 → 0.5 > 0.45

Ordering

Pad every number to the same number of places, then compare place by place.

0.6, 0.06, 0.606 → 0.600, 0.060, 0.606 → 0.06 < 0.6 < 0.606

Adding

Line up the decimal points, not the last digits. Fill gaps with zeros.

3.5 + 12.25 → 3.50 + 12.25 = 15.75

Subtracting

Write a whole number with a point and zeros, then borrow when the top digit is smaller.

5 − 2.37 → 5.00 − 2.37 = 2.63

Multiplying

Multiply without the points, then count the decimal places in the question. The answer gets that many.

0.4 × 0.03 → 4 × 3 = 12 → 3 places → 0.012

Dividing

Move both decimal points the same way until the divisor is a whole number.

3.015 ÷ 0.67 → 301.5 ÷ 67 = 4.5

Estimate first

Before you calculate, round to a quick estimate. If 0.4 × 0.03 came out as 1.2, the estimate (less than 0.4 × 0.1 = 0.04) tells you the point is in the wrong place.
