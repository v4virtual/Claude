Part 1

What counts as a chemical reaction?

A chemical reaction makes one or more new substances. Bonds between atoms in the reactants break, and new bonds form to make the products. No atoms appear and none disappear.

You can't see bonds breaking, so in the lab you look for evidence. One sign alone isn't proof (boiling water also makes bubbles), but several together are strong evidence.

Gas producedFizzing or bubbles, e.g. zinc in acid giving hydrogen
Colour changeCopper turning green as it forms copper carbonate
PrecipitateTwo clear solutions mix and a solid appears
Temperature changeExothermic (gets hot) or endothermic (gets cold)
Light or soundMagnesium burning with a brilliant white flame

Physical change

Same substance, different form. Usually easy to reverse.

  • Melting ice
  • Dissolving salt in water
  • Boiling ethanol

Chemical change

New substance with new properties. Often hard to reverse.

  • Iron rusting (iron oxide forms)
  • Methane burning
  • Baking a cake
Part 2

The law of conservation of mass

In the 1770s and 1780s, the French chemist Antoine Lavoisier, working with Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier, heated metals such as tin inside sealed glass vessels and weighed everything carefully before and after. The total mass did not change. He set the idea out in his 1789 textbook Traité élémentaire de chimie. (Mikhail Lomonosov in Russia had made a similar observation in 1756.)

The law: in a chemical reaction, mass is neither created nor destroyed. The total mass of the reactants equals the total mass of the products.

Why it works: mass comes from atoms. A reaction rearranges atoms into new substances, but the number of each type of atom stays the same. Same atoms, same mass. That's why chemical equations must be balanced.

Part 3 · Interactive

Balance the equation, balance the mass

Change the big numbers in front of each formula (the coefficients). You may never change the small subscripts, because that would make a different substance. When every atom count matches, the mass on both sides matches too.

Particle picture

Mass check

Masses use relative atomic masses (Ar): H = 1, C = 12, O = 16, Na = 23, Mg = 24, Cl = 35.5, Ca = 40, Fe = 56. Grams here are per mole of “equation”, so 2H2 means 2 × 2 = 4 g.

Part 4 · Interactive

“But the mass changed!” Open and closed systems

Sometimes a balance in the lab really does show a different reading after a reaction. The law isn't broken. Something got in or out.

A closed system lets nothing in or out, so the balance reading stays constant. An open system lets gases escape to the air or be taken in from it.

Part 5

Using the law in calculations

Because total mass is conserved, if you know all the masses but one, you can work out the missing one.

Q
0.24 g of magnesium is burned completely in oxygen and forms 0.40 g of magnesium oxide. What mass of oxygen reacted?
1
Write the balanced equation.2Mg + O2 → 2MgO
2
Apply conservation of mass.mass Mg + mass O2 = mass MgO
3
Rearrange and substitute.mass O2 = 0.40 g − 0.24 g = 0.16 g
✓
Check with relative masses: 2 × 24 = 48 g Mg reacts with 32 g O2 to make 80 g MgO. The ratio 48 : 32 : 80 is the same as 0.24 : 0.16 : 0.40. Both multiply by 0.005.

Notice this is exactly the magnesium experiment in Part 4. The 0.16 g “gained” on the balance is the oxygen taken in from the air.

Part 6

Common misconceptions

When wood burns, the mass is destroyed. Only light ash is left.
Most of the mass leaves as carbon dioxide and water vapour. Collect those gases and the total is the same.
Rust is “extra iron”, so iron gets heavier from nothing.
Rust gains mass because oxygen and water from the air join the iron.
Gases have no mass, so they don't count.
Gases are made of atoms and have mass. A litre of carbon dioxide at room temperature is about 1.8 g.
To balance H2 + O2 → H2O, change it to H2O2.
H2O2 is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Only change coefficients: 2H2 + O2 → 2H2O.
Dissolving sugar destroys it, so the mass goes down.
Dissolving is a physical change and mass is conserved here too. Sugar + water = mass of the solution.

Key vocabulary

Reactant
A substance present at the start of a reaction.
Product
A new substance formed by a reaction.
Coefficient
The number in front of a formula showing how many units take part.
Subscript
The small number in a formula showing how many atoms of an element are in one unit.
Closed system
Matter cannot enter or leave; total mass stays constant.
Open system
Matter, especially gases, can enter or leave.
Precipitate
An insoluble solid formed when two solutions react.
Relative formula mass (Mr)
The sum of the relative atomic masses of all atoms in a formula.
